When an AI pet portrait comes out looking generic, the problem is almost never the AI. It's the reference photo. The model can only love the face you show it — and these seven mistakes hide that face. All fixes take about two minutes with a phone. Every claim below comes from regenerating hundreds of portraits in our own studio ([how our AI works](/ai-disclosure)).

## 1. The face is too small in the frame

The number one mistake. A beautiful shot of your dog bounding across a meadow gives the model a face the size of a postage stamp. Crop closer: the face should fill a good third of the frame. If your only photo is a wide shot, crop it before uploading.

![AI-generated example of a strong reference photo: face-on, even light, the face filling the frame — our Pet ID card template](/tpl/idcard.jpg)

## 2. The light is behind your pet

Backlit photos turn faces into silhouettes, and the model guesses fur color in shadow — usually wrong. Stand with your back to the window or shoot outdoors in open shade. If you can see the sparkle in their eyes, the light is right.

## 3. Your pet is mid-motion

Motion blur softens exactly the details the model needs: eye edges, whiskers, fur direction. The result comes out "inspired by" rather than "is". Wait for the sit-stay moment, or use burst mode and pick the sharpest frame.

## 4. Someone's holding the leash (or the pet)

Arms, leashes, hands and half-a-human get blended into the composition. The instruction is simple: the pet alone in the frame. Put the leash down, step back, and shoot.

## 5. Multiple pets in one photo

The model will merge two dogs into one dog that belongs to neither. Photograph each pet separately — every portrait gets its own reference, and you can still commission [a portrait for each of them](/#create).

## 6. The photo is old or low-resolution

A seven-year-old screenshot from a broken phone camera can't carry fine fur detail. Anything above roughly 1000 pixels wide works; screenshots of screenshots don't. If the photo is precious but tiny, upscale it first or retake it — most pets will sit for a fresh shot for a treat.

## 7. The angle is the one you always take

Looking down at a sleeping pet flattens the face. The strongest reference is at your pet's eye level: crouch down, face-on, eyes visible. If you have a second angle — a three-quarter side view — add it; two angles noticeably improve fur and eye fidelity.

## Two questions we hear every day

**"Can I use an old photo?"** Yes — the model works from any clear picture, even a pet who has passed away. Older cameras (pre-2015 phone shots, scanned prints) often lack the fine fur detail the model needs, so expectations should be a little softer, but memorial portraits from archive photos are some of the most meaningful pictures we make.

**"How many photos should I add?"** One perfect photo beats four mediocre ones. If you have a second angle — a side view or a sitting pose — add it and the studio will pull fur and eye detail from both. More than four is unnecessary; the model only needs to learn one face.

## The 30-second checklist

Before you upload, run through it:

- Face fills a third of the frame
- Light on the face, catchlights in the eyes
- Sharp, not mid-blur
- Pet alone, no leash, no hands
- Eye-level angle
